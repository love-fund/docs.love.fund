love.fund grew out of Gitcoin, which has spent 8 years running crowdfunding for public goods.

## By the numbers

- Started in 2017 and ran its first quadratic funding round in 2019.
- More than $60M distributed across 230+ funding rounds: 5M+ donations from 270k unique supporters to 3,715 projects, per [gitcoin.co/impact](https://www.gitcoin.co/impact).
- Yearly volume grew from $0.7M in 2019 to $2.8M in 2020, $13.2M in 2021 and $21.4M in 2022.
- 2024 alone: $10M+ across 105 rounds, from 141.5k donors to 1,743 grantees.
- GG23 (April 2025): $695K to 235 open source projects from 9,991 unique donors.

## Lessons

### Gitcoin 1.0 era: gitcoin.co

1. **Evolve software in conversation with the community.** This is the main lesson. The best product decisions came from shipping, listening and adjusting with the people using it, not from planning in isolation.
2. **Go broad, then go deep.** We built a handful of products, like bounties, hackathons, Kudos and quests, before we landed on Gitcoin Grants. A breadth-first search found what worked; a depth-first search turned it into something people relied on.
3. **The first version is never the one that works.** Even after we found Gitcoin Grants, we had to iterate on it, round after round, to make it useful.
4. **When launching a funding campaign, set an $$$ anchor first.** Matching pools are the anchor point of a crowdfunding campaign.  They are what make quadratic funding work. A round without a funder behind it is just a promise, which is why the first question for any pilot is where the money comes from.
5. **Campaigns have to pay their way.** For years Gitcoin funded public goods out of its own treasury. In 2025 it had to cut spending and shed organizational debt to get back to a stable footing. That's why love.fund campaigns share revenue back.
6. **Curate and fund, and let operators operate.** Gitcoin started to see much more innovation when partners ran their own rounds on its rails, and its 2026 strategy leans on that network: operators run rounds, curators surface signal, capital partners fund. love.fund screens and backs sub-allocators;
7. **Anything that counts people will get gamed.** Quadratic funding rewards how many people give, so it attracts fake accounts. Gitcoin built Passport (now Human Passport) to fight that. Any love.fund mechanism that pays out per person needs a sybil plan from day one.
8. **Trust is the product.** Donors come back when payouts land on time and the reporting is clean. Software and operations decide whether a second round happens.
9. **Quadratic funding is a growth machine, not just a fundraising machine.** Because QF rewards how many people give, every project has a reason to rally its community, and every donor discovers other projects along the way. Lots of people found each other through Gitcoin: builders found funders, collaborators and co-founders. The network it grew was worth as much as the money it moved.
10. **Pair big brains with builders.** Working with thinkers like Vitalik Buterin and Glen Weyl was a gift. They reasoned a priori, from first principles; we were empirical, learning from what actually happened in each round. The mechanism got better because both sides kept talking: theory told us what to try, and the rounds told us what held up.

### Gitcoin 2.0 era: GTC

Lessons from the [GTC retrospective](https://gov.gitcoin.co/t/temp-check-gtc-retrospective/25343), which looks back on the five years since GTC launched in 2021:

1. **Walking out of a corporate parent can work.** Most projects born inside a corporate parent die inside it. Gitcoin spun out of Consensys, launched GTC three months later and handed the project to its community.
2. **Keep the promises you make.** The 2021 launch video promised that GTC holders would govern the treasury, settle disputes, create policy, ratify grants rounds, fund community collections and fight sybils. All of those happened, even where some mechanisms didn't scale.
3. **The category can outgrow its creator.** Quadratic funding spread to Octant, Giveth, Artizen and others. Ideas spread even where the product didn't win.
4. **Building a DAO that works is hard.** We had a lot to learn. 2021–2025 brought hard debates over budgets and direction, reorgs and layoffs, and relationships that still need repair.
5. **Ship, or lose the lead.** The hardest regret: we lost the lead in a market we created, and we didn't ship the software we wanted to ship.
6. **Archive what you can't afford to lose.** When the Gitcoin Media YouTube channel went away, years of talks and educational content went with it.
7. **Markets move under you.** Public goods funding tools declined as ETH stagnated and risk capital moved to AI. A funding model has to survive the cycle it was born in.
8. **Be clear about what a token is.** GTC launched as a governance token with no promised economic value, and that hasn't changed. It's for governance, not a share of anything Gitcoin or love.fund earns.
9. **Name the hurt, then release it.** Repair means naming harms specifically and separating people from circumstances. Forgiveness doesn't mean the decisions were right.

### Gitcoin 2.0 era: Grants Stack

Gitcoin 2.0 bet on software. The [whitepaper](https://web.archive.org/web/20250109040011/https://gitcoin.co/whitepaper) laid out the plan: Grants Stack, a no-code, "enterprise-grade" app for running grants programs, built on Allo Protocol, sold to the "hundreds of DAOs with millions of capital to distribute." It expected that market, then "limited to organizations with token treasuries," to grow "by many orders of magnitude" as more assets moved onchain, and set a north star of funding $1B in grants.

The tools worked, and partners ran real rounds on them. The business didn't:

1. **Size the team to the market you have, not the one you expect.** Web3 grants programs weren't a big enough market to carry a large team. Even a good product in a small market can't pay for a big org.
2. **Don't build on a single customer base that can dry up.** The customers were DAOs funding their ecosystems from token treasuries. When token prices fell and risk capital moved to AI, DAO grant budgets dried up, and so did demand for the tools to run them.
3. **"Orders of magnitude" is a bet, not a plan.** The growth case depended on tokenization reaching far beyond crypto. That may still happen, but it didn't happen on a timeline a company could wait for.
4. **Treasury-funded customers are cyclical.** When the people paying for grants are spending a treasury rather than earning revenue, the grants market rises and falls with token prices.

### Gitcoin 2.0 era: Allo Capital

In August 2024 Kevin laid out a [10-year goal](https://gov.gitcoin.co/t/10-year-goal-allo-could-become-the-capital-allocation-layer-of-the-post-tokenization-internet/19257): "What Stripe did for accepting credit cards, Allo could do for capital allocation." Horizon 1 was to win the grants market and break even by the end of 2025. Horizon 2 was to make Allo the capital allocation layer of a tokenized internet, a market he framed in the trillions. In January 2025 Allo Protocol [spun out of Gitcoin as Allo Capital](https://gov.gitcoin.co/t/announcement-introducing-allo-capital/19839), a standalone company backed by Gitcoin and Protocol Labs. It shipped AlloKit, Deep Funding, Cookie Jar and InfiniteRegen.AI, then pivoted briefly to a venture fund for the Gitcoin ecosystem. In May 2026 it wound down. In Kevin's words, "It didn't work, and I want to be transparent about why."

1. **Cheap experiments on shared rails can beat expensive builds.** The 2024 build table told the story: Gitcoin's own QF stack cost millions and moved about $0.92 of funding per dollar spent. EasyRetroPGF cost $200k and moved about $25 per dollar. Partners built several strategies on Allo at no cost to Gitcoin.
2. **Shipping isn't product-market fit.** Allo Capital shipped four interesting products. None of them reached product-market fit or scalable revenue.
3. **Developer tools are only as good as the developers who want them.** The bet was a protocol plus AlloKit, a toolkit for developers building funding apps onchain. Then the world moved to AI. The builders and the capital went with it, and an onchain toolkit for funding apps stopped being relevant to what most developers were building.
4. **A spinout needs a founder with free attention.** A separate company was meant to buy focus, but Kevin's attention went back to Gitcoin, and the venture suffered for it.
