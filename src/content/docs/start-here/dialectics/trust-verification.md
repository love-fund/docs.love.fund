---
title: Trust x Verification
description: Relationships and proofs are two ways of knowing who to fund. Good systems use proof to extend trust, not replace it.
---

## The tension

Trust is believing in someone because of a relationship: you know them, someone you know vouches for them, or they've shown up before. Verification is checking: proof of identity, proof of work, audits, cryptographic guarantees.

They look opposed. Trust is warm, fast and human, but it doesn't scale beyond the people you know, and it can be exploited or turn into insider networks. Verification scales to strangers and resists fraud, but it can be cold, intrusive and expensive, and it can shut out people who don't fit the checklist.

This is especially relevant to onchain funding, where anyone can create many wallets and Sybil attacks are a constant threat. (For the general method, see [Beyond Dualism](/start-here/beyond-dualism/).)

## The dialectic

- **Thesis: trust.** Relationships are the foundation of community. Treating people as trustworthy, and letting them vouch for each other, is how real communities work.
- **Antithesis: verification.** At scale, trust gets exploited. Without proof, funding goes to whoever games the system best, often at the expense of real people.
- **Synthesis: verifiable trust.** Use proof to extend trust beyond the circle of people you know. Make relationships and reputation legible, so trust can travel without becoming blind.

## What each side protects

| Pole         | What it protects                              | What it becomes alone         |
| ------------ | --------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| Trust        | Relationship, dignity, speed                  | Exploitation and insider clubs |
| Verification | Fairness, scale, resistance to fraud          | Surveillance and exclusion    |

A good synthesis keeps the left column of both rows and avoids the right column of both.

## The assumption to question

The two look incompatible because we assume verification means a central authority checking documents, and trust means having no checks at all.

But relationships themselves can be evidence. Who vouches for whom, who has contributed what, and how long someone has been part of a community are all signals. Systems like web-of-trust, attestations and connection-oriented funding can verify that people are real and distinct by looking at their relationships, without needing an ID card or a gatekeeper.

## What it looks like in practice

- **Vouching.** People who are trusted can bring others in, and are accountable for who they bring.
- **Reputation over time.** Track records and contributions that build trust gradually.
- **Privacy-preserving proof.** Prove you're a unique person or a community member without revealing more than you need to.
- **Plural signals.** Many kinds of evidence, so no single check becomes a gate that excludes people.

## When to hold them apart

Not every case wants a synthesis. Some things should be verified strictly, like large transfers of money and anything involving safety. And some relationships should never be audited: a friendship, a gift between neighbors, care within a family.

The point is to match the level of proof to the stakes.

## The upward spiral

Trust without verification gets exploited. Verification without trust becomes surveillance.

Done well, each one feeds the other. Verification lets trust reach further, and trust makes verification lighter and kinder.
