These are the bets underneath everything love.fund does. If one turns out to be wrong, the strategy should change with it.

## Past

### Networks won

A network business beats every other kind of company. A normal company's value grows with its number of customers; a network's grows with the connections between its users, and it takes a slice of every one. Inside a category, the biggest network ends up larger than everyone else combined, so investors learned to fund only network plays with a credible path to number one.

### Money moved to the virtual, and the physical got debased

Once bits and money moved at the speed of light, returns in the virtual economy beat returns in the physical one, and capital followed. Everything that stayed physical got debased: the local shop, the road, the town. The gains went to a few, the costs were spread wide, and all of that market cap came out of real places.

### Power followed money

Network companies were built on things taxpayers paid for, like roads, telecom and public research, and then grew past the borders of any one country. With tax threats, lobbying, litigation, media and revolving doors, they became more influential over the state than the state is over them.

### The state hollowed out

Government's job shrank to maintenance: fill the potholes, keep the lights on, collect the taxes and enforce the rules of a system most people have no say in. The people who funded the network era called this inevitable, and good news for anyone who got in early. We don't think it was either.

### Three problems

- The virtual ate the physical.
- Power concentrated.
- Synergistic satisfiers became pseudo-satisfiers.

What we want instead: the virtual feeds the physical, so the screen's job is to get you off it and into a place with people. Power is distributed, with every town running its own economy and having its own say, networked to every other town. And the synergistic satisfiers come back: places, like a good bookshop or a town square, that meet several human needs at once.

## Present

### Technology is moving fast

AI is reshaping work faster than people can retrain for it. Big Tech is spending hundreds of billions of dollars a year on compute, while tech companies cut jobs by the thousands.

Crypto spent the last decade building rails that move value across the internet with no bank in the middle. Open source software, cheap hardware and AI tools keep lowering the cost for strangers to build something together.

### The world order is shifting

Trade routes, alliances and supply chains that looked settled ten years ago are being renegotiated in real time. That leaves a lot of people asking which institutions they can still count on, and where they'd go for help if the answer is fewer than they thought.

### Networks can fund things now

For most of modern history, funding anything meant going through an institution built for the industrial era: a bank, a government program, a foundation with a board and an annual grant cycle, a corporate budget. Those institutions make a small number of large decisions slowly, and they ask everyone else to trust the few people inside.

For the first time in history, we can fund things with networks instead. A group of people who may never meet can pool money and decide together where it goes, in public, at internet speed and at any size. Gitcoin has done this for 8 years and moved more than $60M through 230+ rounds that way (see the [track record](/start-here/track-record/)).

### Crypto makes money programmable

Money used to move only as fast as banks and paperwork allowed. Now it can carry rules: match this donation, stream this grant, release this payment when the work is done, send a share upstream. Funding mechanisms that used to need an institution can now be written as software.

### AI makes intelligence commodifiable

Judgment, analysis and allocation used to be scarce and expensive, so only big funders could afford to do them well. AI makes that intelligence cheap and abundant. An everyday donor can now have the kind of research and allocation help that once took a foundation staff.

### atproto lets communities build network effects together

On the old internet, network effects belonged to whichever platform held the data, so every community built someone else's moat. On atproto, people own their identity and their data, and apps share open record types. Every app that adds a vouch, a donation or a relationship makes every other app more useful, and a community can leave without losing its graph. Network effects stop being something a platform captures and become something communities build together and keep. See [Architecture](/roadmap/architecture/).

### Same rails, different incentives

Where we are isn't inevitable. It's the product of incentives to extract as much as possible, push the costs onto someone else and concentrate the power that results. The technology doesn't require those incentives. A different everything app uses the same network math and the same technology, but measures success by needs met instead of time on screen, and pushes wealth and power out to towns and bioregions instead of up to a few owners. That's what love.fund is building.

### The nonprofit industrial complex is ripe for disruption

Much of philanthropy runs through layers of intermediaries, grant cycles and reporting that cost a lot and keep money far from the people doing the work. Programmable money, networks, cheap intelligence and public, transparent funding flows make a more direct path possible.

### The social safety net is being renegotiated right now

The safety nets of the industrial era were built for a world of stable jobs, and that world is going away. Whatever replaces them is being decided now, in policy, in markets and in communities. We want communities to have a real hand in it.

## Future

### An everything app will happen

An app is the small thing you see, and the interface to a very large thing you don't: warehouses, data centers, satellites and the local businesses they replaced. An everything app routes every need, purchase, relationship and vote through one front door. The question is who owns that door.

### An everything app can have shared distribution

On the old internet, every app fought alone for attention, and distribution went to whoever owned the front door. When apps share identity, data and record types, an everything app doesn't have to be one company's product. It can be many apps behind one front door, where a person who arrives for local events can find a gift circle, a funding round or a neighbor's project without starting over. Each app brings people in, and every other app gets to meet them. Distribution becomes something apps build together and share, not something one owner rents out.

### Build the commanding heights, then give them away

The [commanding heights](https://commanding-heights.com) are the chokepoints that control an economy, and the flows that run through them. Whoever controls how AI's economic value flows, not just who builds the intelligence, shapes the next era.

1. Build the commanding heights of the traditional economy.
2. Build the commanding heights of the [sacred economy](/roadmap/opportunity/#commanding-heights-of-a-more-sacred-economy).
3. Build a funnel from 1 to 2.
4. Progressively distribute ownership of the commanding heights in both economies.

See [Opportunity](/roadmap/opportunity/).

### Only build what makes the thing it came from stronger

Technology came from people, from people coordinating in person, and from a living biosphere, so it has to leave all three stronger: healthier people, better in-person coordination and a better biosphere. Anything that grows by debasing what it came from is a cancer. Every feature gets the same test: is the thing it emerged from stronger afterward?

### In the fullness of time, jobs are a pier, not a bridge

We've been told jobs are a bridge: work hard, and they carry you safely to a life of abundance. After AI - and the changes to the social contract in the last 20 years, they look more like a pier: they go out a ways and then they end. As AI automates more work, people need other ways to be held, by each other, by their communities and by funding that follows what they love.

### Relocalizing the economy is an answer

When value circulates close to home, it builds relationships and resilience instead of leaking away. Local and bioregional economies, mutual aid and community-owned commons are part of how people get through what's coming. See [Local x Global](/ontology/dialectics/local-global/).

### Regeneration, not extraction, is the long-term meta

Extraction wins in the short run, and then it runs out of things to extract. Over the long run, the strategies that last are the ones that leave people, places and the living world better off than they found them. See [The Upward Spiral](/ontology/upward-spiral/).

### The future we want is an ancient future

Gift circles, mutual aid, commons and rotating savings kept people fed and held for thousands of years, long before banks, insurers or welfare states. The way forward isn't a return to the past, and it isn't a clean break from it. It's a chance to re-indigenize and re-relationalize ourselves: to remember that an economy is made of relationships, with each other and with the living world, and to rebuild it that way with the tools of this era. And the timing matters: this is happening just as the institutions of the industrial era (stable jobs, pensions, safety nets, trust in big institutions) are decaying. As they fray, the oldest ways of holding each other are what's left, and they're coming back exactly when they're needed. The phrase is Helena Norberg-Hodge's. See [Ancient futures](/roadmap/design-space/#ancient-futures).
