## The tension

Measurement is how we know whether something is working: numbers, outcomes, evidence. Meaning is why the work matters at all: the love, the relationships, the story, the things that make a life or a place worth caring about.

They look opposed. Measurement makes funding accountable, comparable and scalable, but it tends to favor whatever is easy to count, and what gets counted gets gamed. Meaning keeps the work honest about its purpose, but it can be vague, unaccountable, and easy to claim without proof.

This is a real tension for any funding mechanism, including love.fund. The question is how to measure without losing what matters. (For the general method, see [Beyond Dualism](/ontology/beyond-dualism/).)

## The dialectic

- **Thesis: measurement.** If you can't tell whether money is helping, you can't steward it well. Evidence protects funders and communities from waste, fraud and wishful thinking.
- **Antithesis: meaning.** The things people love most, like a friendship, a forest or a tradition, resist metrics. Reduce them to numbers and you lose the point.
- **Synthesis: measurement in service of meaning.** Let people name what matters to them first, in their own words, then use numbers to learn and improve, not to decide what counts.

## What each side protects

| Pole        | What it protects                              | What it becomes alone        |
| ----------- | --------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| Measurement | Accountability, learning, honesty about results | Gaming and Goodhart's law  |
| Meaning     | Purpose, depth, what can't be counted          | Vagueness and unaccountability |

A good synthesis keeps the left column of both rows and avoids the right column of both.

## Goodhart's law

The economist Charles Goodhart noticed a pattern that's usually summed up as: **when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.**

A metric starts out as a signal, a rough way of seeing something you care about. Once money, status or survival depends on it, people start optimizing for the number instead of the thing. The number goes up while the thing it was meant to track stays the same or gets worse.

Funding is full of examples:

- **Count people served** and programs drift toward quick, shallow contact with as many people as possible, rather than the deep work that actually changes lives.
- **Count trees planted** and you get monoculture saplings in the wrong places, many of which die, rather than a living forest.
- **Count votes or donors** in a funding round and people split one wallet into many, or trade votes, to look more popular than they are.
- **Count publications or commits** and you get more papers and more code, not better ones.

None of this requires bad people. It's what happens when a whole system rewards the number. The more pressure on a metric, the faster it stops meaning anything.

Goodhart's law is why measurement can't stand alone. Meaning is what tells you when a number has drifted away from what it was supposed to show. Some ways to resist it:

- **Rotate and refresh metrics** before they get gamed.
- **Use several signals at once**, so improving one at the expense of the others shows up.
- **Keep humans in the loop** who know the work and can tell when the numbers and reality have parted ways.
- **Hold metrics loosely.** Treat them as questions ("why did this change?") rather than verdicts.

## The assumption to question

The two look incompatible because we assume measurement has to be done to people by funders, with metrics chosen from the outside.

But measurement can be done with people. When a community defines its own signals of success, and those signals include stories and relationships alongside numbers, measuring becomes a way of paying attention rather than a way of controlling.

## What it looks like in practice

- **Values first, metrics second.** Ask people what they love and what success looks like to them before choosing what to count.
- **Stories alongside numbers.** Testimony, photos and narratives count as evidence too.
- **Many signals, not one.** A single metric is easy to game. Several, chosen by different people, are harder.
- **Measure to learn, not to rank.** Use data to improve the work, not to sort people into winners and losers.
- **Let love be a signal.** On love.fund, the fact that people choose to fund something is itself information about what matters to them.

## When to hold them apart

Not every case wants a synthesis. Some things should be measured strictly: safety, finances, whether promised money actually arrived. And some things shouldn't be measured at all: a ceremony, a grief, a sacred place. Trying to quantify them can cheapen them.

The point is to know which is which.

## The upward spiral

Measurement without meaning becomes a numbers game. Meaning without measurement becomes unaccountable.

Done well, each one feeds the other. Meaning tells you what to measure, and measurement helps you serve that meaning better.
