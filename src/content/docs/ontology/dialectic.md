## Why a dialectic

A lot of the tensions we live with come as pairs that look opposed: love and money, sovereignty and interdependence, the individual and the collective. We usually treat them as a choice and pick a side.

A dialectic does something else with them. It takes the tension as material to work with. You ask what each side protects, question the assumption that makes them look incompatible, and look for something new that keeps the strengths of both. The answer often turns out to be a design space nobody had explored yet, or a way to turn a zero-sum tradeoff into a loop where each side makes the other stronger.

It has limits. Some differences can't be reconciled, and some values need hard boundaries. A proposed synthesis can also be a tradeoff in disguise. A real one leaves you with something better than either side, which a compromise between two imperfect options doesn't.

Love and money is a good pair to try it on. Love without economic infrastructure struggles to scale. Money without love loses its compass. The aim is to make the tension between them productive rather than pick one.

For more on working with opposites, including when to merge them and when to hold them apart, see [Beyond Dualism](/ontology/beyond-dualism/).

## The dialectic

love.fund starts from a question: what if we could fund what we love, and love what we fund? It works as a dialectic.

- **Thesis: fund what we love.** Let money follow what people actually care about, as they name it themselves.
- **Antithesis: love what we fund.** Funding creates attachment. Sometimes that's good, because attention turns into stewardship. Sometimes it's bad, because we talk ourselves into loving whatever our money is already in.
- **Synthesis: money that flows like metta.** Metta is lovingkindness, and money could move with its properties: unconditional, widening, growing when it's given, practiced instead of transacted.

## The thesis

Point your time, attention and money at what you actually love, rather than at surface wants, because that alignment is what changes a person, and enough changed people change a society.

## Money should serve what you love

Money should also go toward what people value, and not only toward financial return. That makes a society more meaningful and more ethical.

## Real love and surface desire

Real love is care and devotion that doesn't depend on what you get back.

Desire and affection work differently. They rise and fall with how someone behaves and how things turn out.

## Values, preferences and addictions

Start by getting clear on your deepest commitments. Preferences are things you happen to like, and addictions are things that pull at you whether you like them or not. Either one can pass for a value if you don't look closely.

Then check whether your actions match. One test: imagine looking back on your life at its end. Which of this week's choices would you be glad you made?

## Love the thing itself, hold the strategy loosely

Loving an abstract idea like localism is different from loving specific people, specific places or the natural world.

Stay devoted to the core values and let the tactics move. A strategy is a guess about how to serve what you love, and guesses should change when you learn something.

## A six-part economy

Money is one resource among several. The economy has six, and all of them should follow your values:

| Resource   | What it is                                       |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Atoms      | Physical stuff: food, materials, goods           |
| Energy     | Power, fuel, heat                                |
| Bits       | Information and data                             |
| Currency   | Dollars and other money                          |
| Attention  | Your seconds, where your focus actually goes     |
| Creativity | What you make, imagine and build                 |

Each one can flow toward what you love or away from it. Money gets most of the scrutiny, but an hour of attention or a weekend of creative work says something about what you care about too.

## A personal scorecard

The scorecard is a self-assessment: how well do your values match where your time, money and creative effort actually go?

Most of the evidence already exists. Your calendar shows where the hours went and your bank statement shows where the money went; set them next to the list of things you say you love and see how far apart they are.

## Love is infinite, resources are finite

You can love without limit. You can't spend without limit, though, because you only get so many hours, so much energy and so much money.

The clearer you are about what you're committed to, the better you can express that love through those limited channels.

## Dharma

Dharma is an old Sanskrit word for the thing you're here to do: your purpose, your calling.

Purpose stays abstract until it shows up in where your time, attention and money actually go. Point those at what you truly love, and your dharma stops being an idea and becomes the life you're living.
